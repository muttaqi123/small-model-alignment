1. Repository, Models, and Data

Repository. github.com/muttaqi123/small-model-alignment. Core implementation: alignment/batching.py (tokenization, masking), alignment/logprobs.py (response log-probabilities), alignment/losses.py (SFT and DPO objectives), alignment/sft.py and alignment/dpo.py (training loops, no HF Trainer/TRL), alignment/generate.py; submission_adapter.py wraps them. TRL + PEFT are used only in scripts/train_lora_dpo.py. Kaggle notebooks are generated by kaggle/build_notebooks.py; judging runs as a Kaggle Benchmarks task (kaggle/judge_task.py).

Models. Qwen/Qwen2.5-0.5B (base) for SFT and both from-scratch DPO runs; HuggingFaceTB/SmolLM2-360M-Instruct for the LoRA persona task. Seeds. 42 everywhere (dataset shuffles, data-loader order, model init of LoRA, pair-order randomization). Prompt format (SFT, DPO and all Qwen generations): ChatML without a system message, <|im_start|>user\n{instruction}<|im_end|>\n<|im_start|>assistant\n, with the response terminated by <|im_end|>, which we set as the tokenizer's EOS (PAD stays <|endoftext|>). SmolLM2 uses its own chat template, as TRL applies it to the conversational preference records. All datasets are reduced to single-turn examples, shuffled with seed 42, and length-filtered with the training tokenizer so that no training sequence is truncated (UltraChat replies longer than the 512-token budget are dropped rather than cut, which keeps every SFT target ending in EOS).

4. Training Behavior

Figure 1. SFT response-token NLL. Faint: per-step training loss; solid: EMA (0.9); dots: validation loss on 1,000 held-out examples every 50 steps (unsmoothed). Star: selected checkpoint.

SFT. Validation NLL fell from 1.474 (base model, step 0) to 1.378 within 50 steps — mostly learning the ChatML format and when to stop — and then decreased slowly and steadily to 1.317 (perplexity 3.73) at the end of the single epoch. Training and validation losses track each other closely (no overfitting, as expected in one pass over 30k examples), and the lowest validation loss is at the last step, so the best and final checkpoints coincide (step 938). The flat stretch at steps 50–300 is where the 3%-warmup peak LR is still high; the loss resumes falling as the cosine schedule decays. Gradient norms stayed below 7 and no fp16 step was skipped.

Figure 2. From-scratch DPO. Lines: training metrics averaged over each 32-pair optimizer step, EMA 0.9; dots: full validation set every 25 steps (unsmoothed). Stars: checkpoint with the highest validation reward accuracy (ties broken by margin).

DPO. Both runs start exactly at loss log 2 with zero rewards (policy = reference) and learn a consistent preference direction. On UltraFeedback the validation reward accuracy jumps to 0.65 within 25 steps and climbs to 0.70, with the margin growing steadily to 0.66 and the loss falling to 0.570. HH-RLHF is markedly harder: accuracy rises more slowly to 0.65 and the margin only reaches 0.25 (loss 0.631). This matches the nature of the data — HH pairs are often near-duplicates or both mediocre (known label noise), while UltraFeedback pairs differ clearly in quality and length. In both runs the chosen and rejected implicit rewards become negative (e.g. UF final: rw = −0.29, rl = −0.94): DPO mostly lowers the likelihood of rejected responses faster than chosen ones, the well-known “likelihood displacement” effect. With a small fixed LR of 1e-6 for one epoch, neither curve has plateaued and there is no sign of over-optimization (train and validation metrics coincide). Checkpoint selection by validation reward accuracy picked the last step in both runs (UF: 0.704 at steps 450 and 469, tie broken by the larger margin), so best = final. We do not infer response quality from these curves; §5 evaluates generations directly.

Figure 3. TRL DPOTrainer + LoRA on the persona data for three adapter configurations; train loss unsmoothed (thin), held-out metrics every 5 steps.

LoRA-DPO. All three adapters reach 100% held-out reward accuracy within 10 steps — the persona pairs (theatrical, insulting chosen vs. neutral rejected) are trivially separable, so accuracy is uninformative here; the margin and loss distinguish them. The rank-8 attention-only adapter (1.6M parameters) moves slowly (held-out loss 0.456, margin 0.56). Rank 16 on all projections at 5e-5 reaches loss 0.109 / margin 2.28 and flattens by the end of the cosine schedule. At 2e-4 the held-out loss falls below 0.01 by step 25 (0.0008 at the end) and the margin keeps growing to 9.0 — a strong over-optimization signal: the implicit reward of the rejected (neutral, correct) answers falls to −10.1, i.e. their summed log-probability drops by ≈101 nats relative to the reference.

5. DPO-versus-SFT Results

For each DPO track the submitted checkpoint (best = step 469) is compared against the SFT checkpoint on the same prompts, with identical decoding, using blinded pair files (results/eval/pairs/): the response order is randomized per prompt (seed 42) and the judge sees only “Response A/B”. Every pair was judged twice, once in each order, to measure and remove position bias. The safety rubric gives the judge the category's expected behaviour (e.g. “refuse and offer a safe alternative”) and asks it to reward appropriate refusal, privacy protection, calibrated uncertainty and safe redirection while penalizing over-refusal of benign requests; the general rubric scores helpfulness, correctness, instruction-following, concision, formatting and absence of hallucination. Invalid judgments: a pair is re-asked (only the unresolved pairs of a batch) up to 3 times; if a response arrives but never parses to A/B/tie it is “unknown”, if every request fails it is a “failure”; both are excluded from win rates and logged (*_failures.jsonl).

6. Interpretation and Error Analysis

Which model is better, by how much, and why. Measured: on the safety set DPO-HH > SFT by 4–7 points of win rate (54.3% debiased, 56.7% single-order); on AlpacaEval DPO-UF ≈ SFT (48.0–49.2%, not significant); neither DPO model changes the other evaluation set. Interpretation: the HH gain is real but small and uneven across categories (left table): the clearest gains are on medical safety (70%), hate/harassment (63%) and privacy (62%), where DPO-HH more often recommends a professional, redirects to respectful behaviour, or drops the most harmful suggestion; it is worse on cyber abuse (35%) and legal/financial safety (43%). Most notably, 27% of HH pairs are ties in a single order (47% after debiasing), and most of the judge's tie explanations state that both models failed — e.g. both list weapons for “hurt someone in a fight” or write the requested phishing e-mail. DPO with β = 0.1, LR 1e-6 and one epoch moves a 0.5B policy only slightly away from an SFT model that never learned to refuse (UltraChat contains almost no refusals; on the HH set the keyword refusal rate is 2–4% for every model): the preference signal reshapes how it complies (shorter answers, 144 → 119 words, more hedging and referrals) rather than teaching outright refusal. The data also limits the effect: single-turn HH pairs are noisy (many chosen responses are only marginally better), which shows in the low validation margin (0.25) and accuracy (65%) compared with UltraFeedback.

For UltraFeedback the implicit-reward diagnostics were good (validation accuracy 70%, margin 0.66), yet the judged quality did not improve. What changed visibly is form: DPO-UF answers are 35% longer (142 → 191 words) and use lists more often (48% vs 39% of answers), matching the longer, more structured chosen responses of UltraFeedback. But a 0.5B model has little extra knowledge to fill that length: across both orders the judge's explanations mention hallucinated or incorrect content in 144 of 254 DPO-UF losses but in only 76 of 229 wins (keyword count), and on decided pairs the losing DPO-UF answers are the longest relative to SFT (197 vs 129 words). Because our judge was told not to reward length, the stylistic gain that a length-biased evaluator might reward is cancelled by more errors. The result is consistent across AlpacaEval sources (46–52%, right table).

Plausible explanations, tied to evidence. (i) Insufficient optimization: both DPO curves are still improving at step 469, the margins are small, and checkpoint selection picked the last step — longer training or a larger LR/β sweep would likely amplify the effect (not run, compute budget). (ii) Strong but limited SFT baseline: SFT already fixed the dominant failure modes of the base model (looping, never stopping: 34–52% → 1–5% of answers hit the length limit), so the remaining errors are knowledge errors that preference tuning on 15k pairs cannot fix. (iii) Preference-data mismatch: HH pairs reward deflection more than explicit refusal; UltraFeedback rewards length and structure, which helps a larger model more than a 0.5B one. (iv) Judge sensitivity: one third of verdicts flip when the order is swapped, so differences of a few points are at the edge of what 300 prompts can resolve; the order-debiased analysis and the second judge agree on direction. For the persona task the trade-off is explicit: stronger DPO on a tiny style dataset increases persona faithfulness and decreases usefulness/safety monotonically (Table above; the LR 2e-4 adapter insults a user who says they feel hopeless — example below).

7. Limitations and Reproducibility

  • Judge bias and noise. A single LLM judge (gemini-3.6-flash) decides all outcomes. It showed a measurable position preference (see “swap-consistent”), which we neutralize by judging both orders, and moderate agreement with a second judge (gemini-3.7-flash) on a 60-pair sample per comparison. The judge may also favour longer or more confident answers despite the instruction not to.
  • Small evaluations, single seed. 300 prompts per set give a 95% interval of roughly ±5–6 percentage points on a win rate; persona results use only 40 prompts. Each training run was done once (seed 42); seed variance is unmeasured.
  • Compute-bounded recipe. One epoch of DPO at LR 1e-6 is a short run (469 steps); longer training or a larger β/LR sweep was not attempted within the Kaggle budget. The HH evaluation prompts are synthetic and templated; the AlpacaEval subset is judged pairwise against SFT, not with the official length-controlled AlpacaEval protocol.
  • Heuristic statistics. The refusal-phrase rate is a keyword heuristic, used only to describe behaviour.
